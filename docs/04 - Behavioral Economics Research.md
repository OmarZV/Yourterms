# 04 - Behavioral Economics Research

## Executive Summary

Traditional finance assumes investors generally make rational decisions based on available information.

Behavioral economics demonstrates that real-world decision-making is more complicated.

Investors are influenced by cognitive biases, emotions, social pressures, and the way information is presented. These forces can cause individuals to make decisions that conflict with their own long-term financial goals.

For Yourterms, this creates a fundamental product opportunity:

> **If technology can recognize the behavioral forces influencing an investment decision, it can help investors reflect before acting.**

Yourterms applies principles from behavioral economics to create an AI-powered decision layer designed to help investors identify biases, challenge assumptions, and make more deliberate investment decisions.

# Core Behavioral Biases

## 1. Loss Aversion

### What It Is

People tend to experience the psychological impact of losses more strongly than equivalent gains.

An investor may therefore behave differently toward an investment simply because it is currently showing a loss.

### Example

An investor purchases a stock at $100.

The stock falls to $70.

Rather than evaluating the company based on its current fundamentals and future prospects, the investor refuses to sell because selling would make the loss feel "real."

### Investor Risk

Loss aversion can contribute to:

- Holding losing investments too long
- Avoiding necessary portfolio changes
- Taking excessive risk to recover losses
- Making decisions based on purchase price rather than current information

### Yourterms Opportunity

Yourterms can recognize when a user's reasoning is heavily anchored to avoiding a realized loss.

Instead of saying:

> "Sell the stock."

The AI could ask:

> "If you didn't already own this investment, would you buy it today at its current price?"

This reframes the decision around current information rather than the emotional weight of the original purchase.

---

## 2. Anchoring

### What It Is

People often rely disproportionately on an initial piece of information when evaluating subsequent information.

For investors, common anchors include:

- Purchase price
- Previous stock highs
- Analyst price targets
- Historical valuations
- Round-number price levels

### Example

A stock previously traded at $200 but now trades at $120.

An investor may automatically perceive $120 as "cheap" because they are comparing it with the previous $200 price.

The previous price, however, does not necessarily indicate the company's current intrinsic value.

### Investor Risk

Anchoring can cause investors to:

- Assume previous highs will return
- Hold investments waiting to "break even"
- Interpret price declines as opportunities without examining fundamentals
- Give excessive weight to arbitrary reference points

### Yourterms Opportunity

The product can identify the user's reference point and challenge whether it remains relevant.

> "You mentioned that the stock previously traded at $200. What has changed about the business since then?"

---

## 3. Confirmation Bias

### What It Is

People naturally seek, interpret, and remember information that supports their existing beliefs.

In investing, this can become particularly dangerous because investors can easily find opinions supporting almost any investment thesis.

### Example

An investor believes a company is significantly undervalued.

They repeatedly consume:

- Bullish analyst reports
- Positive social media posts
- Favorable earnings commentary
- Supporting valuation arguments

At the same time, they discount information that contradicts their thesis.

### Investor Risk

Confirmation bias can result in:

- Incomplete investment research
- Excessive conviction
- Ignoring warning signs
- Failure to update an investment thesis
- Concentrated positions

### Yourterms Opportunity

Yourterms should deliberately introduce **counterarguments**.

The system could ask:

> "What evidence would convince you that your investment thesis is wrong?"

It could then surface evidence both **supporting and challenging** the user's reasoning.

### Product Principle

> **The AI should challenge the investor, not simply agree with them.**

---

## 4. Herd Behavior

### What It Is

People frequently use the behavior of others as a signal for what they should do.

Financial markets amplify this tendency because investors constantly observe the actions and opinions of other market participants.

### Example

An investment rapidly increases in popularity across financial media and social networks.

An investor becomes interested primarily because:

> "Everyone seems to be buying it."

### Investor Risk

Herd behavior can contribute to:

- Buying after large price increases
- Speculative bubbles
- Panic selling
- FOMO
- Abandoning an existing investment strategy

### Yourterms Opportunity

When social proof appears to be driving a decision, Yourterms can separate the **investment thesis from market popularity**.

For example:

> "If you hadn't seen other investors discussing this company, would you still be interested in owning it?"

---

## 5. Recency Bias

### What It Is

People tend to place disproportionate importance on recent events when predicting the future.

### Example

The stock market has performed strongly for several months.

An investor begins increasing portfolio risk because recent performance makes continued gains feel increasingly likely.

The reverse can occur following market declines.

### Investor Risk

Recency bias can lead to:

- Performance chasing
- Buying near market peaks
- Selling following downturns
- Constantly changing investment strategies
- Underestimating long-term historical patterns

### Yourterms Opportunity

Yourterms can expand the investor's time horizon.

Instead of focusing exclusively on recent performance, the product can contextualize the decision against:

- Longer historical periods
- Previous market cycles
- The investor's original time horizon
- Their stated investment objectives

---

## 6. Overconfidence

### What It Is

Individuals often overestimate the accuracy of their knowledge, predictions, or ability to control outcomes.

Investing provides frequent opportunities for this bias because successful outcomes can easily be attributed to skill rather than luck.

### Example

After several successful trades, an investor concludes that they have developed an ability to identify short-term market opportunities.

They begin:

- Trading more frequently
- Increasing position sizes
- Concentrating their portfolio
- Taking greater risks

### Investor Risk

Overconfidence can contribute to:

- Excessive trading
- Under-diversification
- Excessive position sizing
- Underestimating uncertainty
- Confusing favorable outcomes with good decision-making

### Yourterms Opportunity

Yourterms can separate **decision quality from outcome quality**.

A profitable trade was not necessarily a good decision.

A losing investment was not necessarily a bad decision.

The system should help users evaluate:

> "Was the reasoning behind the decision sound given the information available at the time?"

---

## 7. Disposition Effect

### What It Is

Investors may be more likely to sell investments that have gained value while continuing to hold investments that have declined.

This behavior can result from the combination of loss aversion and the desire to experience the satisfaction of realizing a gain.

### Example

An investor owns two stocks:

**Stock A:** +30%  
**Stock B:** -30%

They sell Stock A because they want to "lock in the gain" while holding Stock B because they want to "wait until it comes back."

Neither decision necessarily reflects the future prospects of the companies.

### Investor Risk

The disposition effect can result in:

- Selling strong investments prematurely
- Holding deteriorating investments too long
- Tax inefficiency
- Portfolio decisions driven by historical returns

### Yourterms Opportunity

The product can evaluate each investment independently from its gain/loss status.

> "Ignoring what you originally paid, which investment has the stronger thesis today?"

---

## 8. Sunk Cost Fallacy

### What It Is

People sometimes continue with a decision because of resources already committed, even when those resources cannot be recovered.

### Example

An investor continues holding or adding to an investment because:

> "I've already lost too much to sell now."

The previous loss becomes part of the justification for committing additional capital.

### Investor Risk

Sunk-cost thinking can lead investors to:

- Double down on deteriorating investments
- Refuse to reconsider previous decisions
- Allocate capital based on past losses
- Escalate commitment to a failing thesis

### Yourterms Opportunity

Yourterms can reframe the question:

> "If this money were sitting in cash today, would this still be where you would choose to invest it?"

# Behavioral Decision Framework

The research suggests that Yourterms should not treat behavioral finance as a library of educational articles.

Behavioral intelligence should exist **inside the decision experience itself.**

A potential decision framework:

### Step 1 — Capture the Decision

The investor explains what they are considering.

> "I'm thinking about selling Apple."

### Step 2 — Understand the Reasoning

The AI explores why.

> "What's making you consider selling now?"

### Step 3 — Detect Potential Behavioral Signals

The system evaluates the reasoning for patterns associated with:

- Loss aversion
- Anchoring
- Confirmation bias
- Herd behavior
- Recency bias
- Overconfidence
- Disposition effect
- Sunk-cost thinking

### Step 4 — Introduce Friction

Rather than immediately providing an answer, Yourterms creates a deliberate reflection moment.

### Step 5 — Challenge the Thesis

The AI introduces alternative interpretations and counterarguments.

### Step 6 — Reconnect With Goals

The decision is evaluated against the investor's:

- Financial goals
- Time horizon
- Risk tolerance
- Portfolio strategy
- Original investment thesis

### Step 7 — Decision

The investor makes the final decision.

### Step 8 — Learn

Yourterms records the reasoning behind the decision so the investor can revisit it later.

# Behavioral Decision Journal

One of the strongest long-term opportunities is creating a record of **why an investor made each decision**.

Before executing or finalizing a decision, the user could record:

- What they are considering
- Why they are considering it
- Their confidence level
- Their expected outcome
- Their time horizon
- Risks they identified
- Potential behavioral biases detected by Yourterms

Over time, this creates a personalized **decision history**.

The AI could eventually identify patterns such as:

> "You've considered selling during four major market declines. In three of those cases, your original investment thesis had not changed."

or:

> "Your confidence tends to increase after an investment has recently performed well."

This moves personalization beyond knowing what an investor owns.

It begins to understand **how the investor makes decisions.**

# Product Implications

Behavioral research suggests several requirements for Yourterms.

### 1. The AI Should Not Immediately Answer

Instant answers can reinforce impulsive decision-making.

For high-impact investment decisions, the experience should sometimes introduce deliberate friction.

### 2. The AI Should Be Willing to Disagree

A useful investing assistant cannot simply optimize for user agreement.

It must be capable of respectfully challenging assumptions.

### 3. Historical Decisions Are Valuable Data

Understanding previous decisions can enable increasingly personalized behavioral insights.

### 4. Context Matters More Than Generic Advice

The same market event can rationally lead to different decisions for investors with different goals and time horizons.

### 5. Uncertainty Should Be Visible

Financial decisions rarely have certain outcomes.

The product should communicate uncertainty rather than presenting AI-generated conclusions as facts.

### 6. Success Should Not Be Measured by Investment Returns Alone

Market outcomes contain randomness.

Yourterms should distinguish between:

**Good Outcome ≠ Good Decision**

and

**Bad Outcome ≠ Bad Decision**

The product should optimize for **decision quality**, not short-term portfolio performance.

**Reinforce not to sweat it and leverage gamification positively**

The product should leverage gamification positively to ensure long term gains not short term trading

# Key Research Insight

Most financial technology products know:

- What an investor owns
- What an investor trades
- How much money they have
- What markets they follow

Yourterms has an opportunity to understand something different:

> **Why the investor makes the decisions they make.**

That behavioral layer can become the foundation for increasingly personalized AI guidance.

# Product Thesis

> **The greatest opportunity for AI in retail investing may not be predicting what the market will do next, but helping investors understand how they themselves are likely to respond to it.**

Yourterms applies behavioral economics not as an educational feature, but as a core component of the product's decision intelligence.
